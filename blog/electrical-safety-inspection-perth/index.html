Electrical safety inspection Perth: what we check, what we find, what you keep

When did you last actually look at the wiring? Not “the lights work” — actually look at it. Most Perth homes go a decade between full electrical inspections. Winter surfaces the problems: boards trip, RCDs won’t reset, smoke alarms chirp at 3am. Here’s exactly what a whole-home inspection covers, what we typically find, and what you keep on the day.

Why Perth winter surfaces electrical problems

Summer hides electrical faults. Cool ambient temperature, low overall load, aircon running on cool mode where the compressor cycles gently. Winter does the opposite. Heaters running, aircon in heat mode drawing peak current, extra lighting, oven on more often for warm meals, kettle boiling constantly. The whole electrical system is working harder for longer.

That’s when the marginal problems become real problems:

  • Older switchboards that were fine at 30°C ambient start showing heat damage at connections when peak current runs through them.
  • Aged RCDs that used to reset first go start requiring multiple attempts, then stop resetting at all.
  • Marginal smoke alarms that chirped occasionally start chirping constantly — usually because the sensor has drifted or the backup battery is dying.
  • Surge damage from storm season shows up weeks later as devices that mysteriously stop working or run hot.
  • Outdoor fittings that survived the summer take a beating from Perth’s rain and start conducting through cracked seals.

Booking an inspection in July or August catches these under load, while the failures are visible, rather than after they cause an outage or a fire.

What a full inspection covers

A whole-home electrical safety inspection is not a walk-through. It’s a documented systematic check of every major electrical system in the property. Here’s the full scope:

  • Switchboard and main switch — overall condition, heat damage, terminal tightness, main switch rating vs. the current household load, spare positions, age of the board itself, presence of ceramic fuses (a red flag).
  • Circuit breakers — each MCB checked, labelling audited (many switchboards have wrong labels that make emergencies harder).
  • RCDs and safety switches — every RCD trip-time tested against AS/NZS 3760, age noted, coverage checked against WA regulations (all final subcircuits must be RCD-protected on any home sold or leased since 2009).
  • Smoke alarms — every alarm inspected for age (10-year lifespan under AS 3786), type (photoelectric preferred, ionisation phased out), placement per Building Code of Australia, interconnection between alarms, and 240V hardwiring where required.
  • Surge protection — whether the property has whole-house surge protection, status indicator check if fitted.
  • Earthing and bonding — earth stake or equivalent, main earth conductor, water pipe bonding, MEN link.
  • Visible wiring and junction boxes — anywhere accessible (roof cavity if safe, sheds, cabinets under sinks, meter box).
  • Outdoor and weatherproof fittings — every external GPO, sensor light, garden light, hot water isolator, aircon isolator, EV charger, solar isolator. IP rating checked, corrosion noted.
  • Ceiling fans and hard-wired appliances — visible mounting, no loose or exposed conductors.
  • Meter box and mains — overall condition, tails to switchboard, Western Power seal integrity.

What we typically find in Perth homes

Perth housing stock is a mix of ages and standards. Here are the issues we see most often, by home era:

Homes built pre-1985

  • Ceramic-fuse switchboards still in service. Slow to trip, no RCD coverage, terminals often loose.
  • No RCDs at all or a single RCD on one circuit only. Under current WA regulations, all final subcircuits must be RCD-protected on any home sold or leased since 2009 — but the home itself may never have been sold in that window.
  • Original 1970s smoke alarms if any (usually none). Battery-only ionisation.
  • Aluminium mains cabling in some cases — requires careful termination checking.
  • Meter box mounted on the fibro exterior with visible weathering.

Homes built 1985-2005

  • First-generation RCDs now well past their reliable service life (10-15 years). Trip times drift, some no longer trip at all.
  • Circuit breakers for lighting/power split, but often no dedicated circuits for major appliances added later (aircon, oven, EV charger).
  • Smoke alarms retrofitted in a mix of positions — often battery-only, sometimes non-compliant type.
  • Outdoor fittings with cracked or brittle plastic covers after 20+ years of UV.

Homes built 2005-2020

  • Original RCDs approaching or past 10-year mark — still trip on test but reset time increasing.
  • 240V smoke alarms present but often original from build, past their 10-year expiry.
  • Switchboard now tight on space after solar, aircon and EV charger additions over the years.
  • Surge protection almost never installed — a gap given the value of connected electronics.

Modern builds (post-2020)

  • Generally compliant on RCDs and 240V smoke alarms at handover.
  • Owner-added work (garden lighting, sheds, extensions, EV charger) sometimes DIY and non-compliant — this is where the risk sits.
  • Surge protection still commonly missing.

Pre-purchase electrical inspections

Buying an older Perth home? A pre-purchase electrical inspection tells you what you’re actually buying — before settlement, not after. The seller isn’t legally required to disclose most electrical issues, and the standard building inspection report almost never covers electrical in real depth.

What comes up on pre-purchase inspections most often:

  • Missing or non-compliant RCDs — the seller must remedy before settlement in WA (RCD and smoke alarm regulations apply on transfer of ownership).
  • Smoke alarms past their 10-year expiry — likewise must be replaced.
  • Switchboards close to end-of-life that will need upgrading within a year or two.
  • DIY work in sheds, granny flats, garden lighting that the seller may not even know about.
  • Water damage in the meter box or main switchboard from an old leak.

The signed inspection report gives you documented negotiating leverage. In some cases the vendor pays for remediation before settlement. In others, the price gets adjusted. Either way, you go in with your eyes open.

Landlord and rental property audits

Under the Residential Tenancies Act 1987 (WA) and the associated electrical safety and smoke alarm regulations, WA landlords have specific ongoing electrical obligations:

  • RCD coverage on all final subcircuits. If it wasn’t retrofitted when the property was first leased or sold post-2009, it’s required now.
  • 240V mains-powered, interconnected photoelectric smoke alarms in every bedroom and on every level, replaced within 10 years of manufacture.
  • Reasonable maintenance of electrical installations throughout the tenancy — not just at handover.

A landlord audit inspection documents all of this in one visit and produces a signed compliance report that goes into the property file. If a tenant is later injured by an electrical fault, having a recent audit report is the single strongest evidence that the property was maintained to standard.

For portfolio landlords or agencies managing multiple properties, we run these inspections in batches with a consolidated compliance report per property. Talk to us about property manager arrangements.

The signed compliance report

Every inspection produces a formal written report you keep. Included:

  • Cover page — property address, inspection date, licensed electrician name and EC009022 licence number, our signature.
  • Systems checked — each of the 10 areas above, with a status for each: compliant, needs attention, or urgent.
  • Findings with photos — anything of note documented with an image and a plain-English description.
  • Recommendations with prices — fixed prices for anything we suggest, held for 90 days from inspection date.
  • Compliance status — whether the property currently meets WA electrical requirements for owner-occupation or lease, referenced against relevant regulations.

Keep it with your home insurance documents and any conveyancing paperwork. It’s the strongest evidence you have that the electrical system was checked to standard on a specific date — useful for insurance claims, future sale disclosure, and rental compliance records.

Common questions about electrical safety inspections

How long does a whole-home electrical safety inspection take?+

Typically 90 minutes to two hours on site for a standard three or four bedroom Perth home. Bigger properties, homes with sheds, granny flats or extensive outdoor lighting take longer. We test every RCD, walk every room, open the switchboard, check every smoke alarm, and inspect every outdoor fitting. Signed compliance report ready by the end of the visit.

Do I need to be home during the inspection?+

Preferably yes for the first inspection — there are usually a few questions about switchboard history, past electrical work, or which appliances are new. That said, we routinely do inspections for landlords and property managers where the tenant is home instead. We can also work with a leased-out property using standard entry-notice arrangements. The signed report goes to whoever booked the inspection.

What if you find something urgent during the inspection?+

If we find something that’s an immediate safety risk — exposed conductors, a scorched switchboard, a failed RCD on a wet-area circuit — we’ll flag it, isolate it if possible, and offer to make it safe on the spot at a fixed price. Nothing gets fixed without your agreement. If it’s not urgent, it goes on the recommendation list with a fixed price beside it and you decide what to book in.

Are electrical safety inspections required by law in WA?+

For owner-occupied homes, whole-home inspections aren't legally required, but some individual elements are: RCDs on all final subcircuits and mains-powered interconnected smoke alarms are both required when a property changes hands or is rented out under WA law. For rental properties, landlords have ongoing obligations under the Residential Tenancies Act 1987 (WA). A safety inspection is the cleanest way to confirm you're compliant and to document it.

How often should I get a whole-home electrical inspection?+

The general recommendation for owner-occupied Perth homes is every 10 years, or immediately after purchasing an older home. For rental properties, at every change of tenant is sensible. Homes with older switchboards, aluminium wiring, or that have had DIY work done should be inspected sooner. If you've had unexplained trips, flickering lights, or smoke alarm faults, don't wait for the 10-year mark.

Does the inspection include solar or EV charger checks?+

Yes, if you have them. The inspection checks how the solar inverter and EV charger integrate with the switchboard, whether the dedicated circuits are correctly protected, and whether surge protection is adequate for the added grid-connected electronics. Panel-level solar servicing and inverter deep-diagnostics are separate services, but the safety inspection catches issues at the switchboard interface where most problems occur.

What's the difference between an inspection report and an Electrical Safety Certificate?+

The safety inspection produces a written report of everything we checked, what we found, and priority-rated recommendations. If we perform any electrical work on the day (or later), that work is separately covered by a Notice of Completion and an Electrical Safety Certificate as required by EnergySafety WA. So an inspection alone gives you the report; if any work follows, you additionally get the compliance certificates for that work.
