The Perth smoke alarm 10-year rule every homeowner should know

Turn one of your smoke alarms over and look at the date on the back. Every alarm sold in Australia is stamped with a manufacture date, and every alarm needs to be replaced ten years after that date — whether it beeps, whether it’s dusty, whether the battery is fresh. The ten-year rule — why the date on the back matters

Every smoke alarm has a use-by date. It’s not marketing — it’s the manufacturer’s specification and the Australian Standard (AS 3786-2014). The sensor inside a smoke alarm slowly degrades with age, dust exposure, and normal wear. After ten years from the manufacture date, the alarm can no longer be relied on to detect smoke early enough to matter.

The important part: the ten years run from the manufacture date, not from when it was installed. An alarm that sat in a hardware store for two years and then went into your ceiling has only eight years of real life left. If you’ve lived in the house since it was built and it’s over ten years old, your alarms are almost certainly past their use-by date — even if they still chirp when you press the test button.

How to check yours right now: twist the alarm off its ceiling base (they usually rotate anti-clockwise), turn it over, and look for a printed label. The manufacture date is stamped there — sometimes as a full date, sometimes as a month/year, sometimes as a batch code with the year encoded. If you can’t find a date at all, or if the alarm is over ten years old, replace it.

What WA law requires for smoke alarms

Three separate rules, all of which sit on top of the ten-year rule above.

1. Mains-powered (hardwired) at transfer of ownership and in rentals

The Building Regulations 2012 (WA) require mains-powered smoke alarms — not battery-only — whenever a home is sold, and in every rental property. A hardwired alarm is connected to the lighting circuit with a small backup battery for power outages. Battery-only alarms don’t meet the standard for sale or rental. If you’re listing or renting out a home with battery-only alarms, they need to be replaced with mains-powered before settlement or the next tenancy.

2. Interconnected in builds and major renovations since 1 May 2015

Homes built or significantly renovated since 1 May 2015 must have alarms that are interconnected — when one detects smoke, they all sound. The reasoning: if a fire starts in a rear bedroom, an alarm in the front hallway is no help if you can’t hear it. Modern interconnect can be hardwired or wireless. Older homes that haven’t been renovated don’t have this obligation, but we recommend it anyway for larger houses.

3. Photoelectric — DFES-recommended, and what we fit as standard

DFES recommends photoelectric smoke alarms, and they’re what we fit as standard on every job. Photoelectric detects smouldering fires much faster — and smouldering fires are the type most likely to occur in a Perth home overnight (electrical faults, cushions, mattresses). Older ionisation alarms are being phased out. Every alarm we install is photoelectric.

Common misconception

“Battery-only is fine because it works in a blackout.” The mains-powered alarms we install have a small backup battery built in — they work in a blackout too, and they meet the standard. You get the best of both.

Where alarms go — and how many

For a standard house (Class 1a under the Building Code), WA law sets the minimum by location, not by a fixed count. Mains-powered alarms are required:

  • in the corridor or hallway that serves the bedrooms — or, if there’s no hallway, in a spot between the bedrooms and the rest of the home; and
  • on any storey that doesn’t contain bedrooms (for example, a lower floor with only living areas).

WA law does not require an alarm inside every bedroom. That said, DFES recommends a photoelectric alarm in each bedroom as best practice — a fire that starts behind a closed bedroom door can take longer to reach a hallway alarm — and we’re happy to fit them wherever you want the extra cover.

One exception — Class 1b

Boarding houses, guest houses and similar short-stay accommodation (Class 1b) have stricter requirements, including alarms inside the bedrooms. If that’s your property, tell us on the call and we’ll scope it correctly.

What NOT to do: don’t put a smoke alarm in the kitchen or bathroom — steam and cooking cause constant nuisance alarms, and a nuisance alarm is the number-one reason people disconnect a unit and never put it back. The kitchen is better covered by a heat alarm, which reacts to a rapid rise in temperature rather than smoke, so it won’t trip on cooking; DFES recommends heat alarms for kitchens and garages as best practice. Beyond that, don’t place smoke alarms within 300mm of a wall or corner (dead air zones), or within 400mm of a ceiling fan or air conditioner vent (airflow disrupts detection). We work out the correct locations during the walkthrough.

Landlord, seller, or owner-occupier — what applies to you

Landlords

Mains-powered alarms compliant with AS 3786 must be installed in every rental property. Alarms must be tested at every change of tenancy and any alarm past its ten-year date must be replaced before the next tenancy begins. Non-compliance carries penalties and can affect insurance in the event of a fire. Most property managers now request a compliance certificate at each renewal — we issue these on the day.

Sellers listing this spring

Settlement agents will ask for smoke alarm compliance. If your alarms are battery-only or past the ten-year date, they need to be replaced before transfer of ownership. Sort it before you list — not the day of settlement.

Owner-occupiers

There’s no legal transfer or tenancy trigger for owner-occupiers, but the ten-year rule still applies to the physical alarm regardless of legal status. A dead alarm won’t save your life in a fire. If yours are over ten years old, replace them.

Common questions about Perth smoke alarms

Where do I find the manufacture date on my smoke alarm?+

Twist the alarm off its base and turn it over. There will be a printed label on the back with the manufacture date — usually shown as month/year or a full date stamp. Every smoke alarm sold in Australia must have this label. If you can’t find a date at all, the alarm is either very old or non-compliant — replace it.

Do I really have to replace an alarm that still beeps and works?+

Yes. The sensor inside a smoke alarm degrades over time even if the alarm still sounds when tested. AS 3786 and manufacturer specifications require replacement ten years from the manufacture date — regardless of whether it beeps, has a fresh battery, or looks fine.

What does WA law actually require for smoke alarms?+

Mains-powered (hardwired) alarms at transfer of ownership and in every rental property, positioned in the hallway serving the bedrooms and on any storey without bedrooms. Interconnected in homes built or renovated since 1 May 2015. Photoelectric is DFES-recommended and what we fit as standard. Battery-only doesn’t meet the standard for sale or rental.

Photoelectric vs ionisation — which one?+

Photoelectric. DFES recommends photoelectric alarms because they detect smouldering fires much faster — the type most likely in a Perth home overnight (electrical faults, cushions, mattresses). Ionisation alarms are being phased out. We only fit photoelectric.

How many alarms do I need, and where?+

For a standard house (Class 1a), WA law requires mains-powered alarms in the hallway serving the bedrooms (or between the bedrooms and the rest of the home if there’s no hallway), plus one on any storey without bedrooms. Alarms inside bedrooms aren’t required by law, but DFES recommends them as best practice. Boarding and short-stay houses (Class 1b) have stricter rules. We work out the exact locations during the walkthrough.

I’m a landlord — what are my obligations?+

Mains-powered AS 3786 alarms in every rental. Tested at every change of tenancy. Any alarm past its ten-year date must be replaced before the next tenancy. We issue compliance certificates for property managers on the day.

I’m selling this spring — when should I sort this out?+

Before you list. Settlement agents ask for smoke alarm compliance and non-compliance discovered late can hold up settlement. We check, replace and certify in one visit and give you the paperwork.
