Home EV charger Perth: 2026 install rules and what your home needs

EV adoption in Perth is climbing fast — more homes added one in the past 12 months than in the previous 10 combined. If you’ve got an EV, your home charger might not be set up properly. If you’re thinking about one, your home isn’t necessarily ready. And the rules just changed in November 2025. Here’s what AS/NZS 3000 Amendment 3 now requires, which brands we install, and what most pre-2025 setups got wrong.

What AS/NZS 3000 Amendment 3 changed in November 2025

The Australian wiring rules got an update in late 2025 specifically aimed at home EV charging. AS/NZS 3000 Amendment 3 took effect in November and added three concrete requirements for new home charger installs. Older installs done before that date were compliant under the previous rules — but the goalposts moved.

The three new requirements:

  • Demand-response capability on chargers above 20A single-phase — mandatory
  • Dedicated circuit, separately RCD-protected — no more sharing with the oven, aircon or general power
  • Switchboard capacity check before install — documented as part of the certificate of compliance

Each of these matters for a specific reason. The next three sections cover them one at a time.

Demand-response: what it is and why it’s now required

Demand-response is the feature that lets the electricity network communicate with your charger and ease its load during peak demand periods — typically 5 to 9pm on hot summer days or cold winter mornings. The charger automatically reduces its draw or pauses charging until peak passes, then resumes.

Two reasons it’s now mandatory above 20A single-phase:

  • Grid stability. As more Perth households add EVs, the simultaneous evening peak (kids home, cooking, heating, plus everyone plugging in their car) starts straining the local network. Demand-response means the grid can manage that surge without blackouts.
  • Lower-cost charging windows. Some retailers now offer time-of-use tariffs that make off-peak charging substantially cheaper. A demand-response-capable charger can take advantage of these — older chargers without the feature can’t.

The good news: all four major brands we install (Tesla Wall Connector, Fronius Wattpilot, ABB Terra AC, Schneider EVlink) are demand-response capable out of the box. If you’re upgrading from a pre-2024 charger, a swap to a current model is usually the cleaner fix — rather than trying to retrofit demand-response onto an older unit.

Dedicated circuit and RCD protection: no more sharing

Pre-Amendment 3 it was common to wire an EV charger off an existing circuit — sometimes sharing with the oven, sometimes off a general power circuit. That doesn’t fly anymore. The new rules require:

  • A dedicated circuit running from the switchboard to the charger location — nothing else on it
  • Its own RCD separate from the rest of the house — so if the charger trips, the kitchen and lights stay on
  • Correctly sized cable for the charger’s current draw (typically 32A for a 7.4kW single-phase charger, larger for higher-rated units)

Why the change? Two real-world failure modes. First, on a shared circuit, an EV charger drawing 32A continuously for hours can degrade the wiring and overload other appliances. Second, a charger fault on a shared RCD takes down half the house — including the lights you need to investigate what tripped. Dedicated and separate fixes both.

If you’re curious about RCD requirements for the rest of the home, our RCD testing in Perth guide covers the WA legal minimum and the 10-second home owner test the government recommends.

Switchboard capacity: the bit most people don’t know to check

This is the requirement that catches the most surprise on retrofits. Your home’s consumer mains supply (the cable coming in from the street) has a fixed capacity — usually 50A, 63A or 80A for older homes, sometimes 100A for newer ones. Adding a 32A EV charger on top of the existing aircon, oven, hot water and lighting can push the total above what the mains can handle.

Four brands cover 95% of the Perth home EV charger market. All four meet AS/NZS 3000 Amendment 3 and support demand-response. The choice usually comes down to which EV you own and which ecosystem you want.

Tesla Wall Connector

The natural pick if you own a Tesla — tightest integration with the car, automatic charge scheduling via the Tesla app, supports up to 22kW on three-phase. Works with non-Tesla EVs too via the J1772/Type 2 cable, but the smart features are Tesla-specific. Clean modern design.

Strongest pick if you have or plan solar PV. The Wattpilot integrates with Fronius solar inverters to surplus-charge from your own generation — the car charges only when there’s spare solar production. Big saving on charging costs if your home is solar-equipped. Works with any EV via Type 2.

ABB Terra AC

The workhorse choice for non-Tesla EVs, rental properties, and households who want a reliable charger without smart-home complexity. Broad EV compatibility, simple installation, strong warranty, no app required for basic use (though available if you want it).

Best for homes that already have Schneider smart-home gear (KNX, Wiser) or want full smart-home integration with app control, scheduling, and load management. Slightly higher price point than ABB but the smart features are the trade-off.

We’ll talk through the trade-offs when you ring — it’s not a one-size-fits-all decision. The right charger depends on the car, your home setup, and whether you have solar.

Common mistakes from pre-2025 installs we fix on retrofits

Four patterns we see regularly when checking older home EV charger setups:

1

Charger on a shared circuit with the oven or aircon

Common in 2020-2024 installs. The simplest path was to tee off an existing high-current circuit. Doesn’t meet the current standard. We separate the charger onto its own circuit with its own RCD.

2

No demand-response capability

Pre-2024 chargers often don’t support demand-response at all. The charger itself needs replacing with a current model to comply. Usually we can reuse the cabling and circuit if those were sized correctly.

3

Switchboard not checked, capacity marginal

Many older installs were done without a proper capacity calculation. The charger works fine in winter but trips the main when the household runs aircon + EV charger + oven on a summer evening. Fix is a switchboard upgrade or load management.

4

Wrong cable size for the charger’s rated current

If a 32A charger was wired with 6mm cable instead of 10mm, the cable runs hot under continuous load. Common in DIY-ish installs from before licensed-only enforcement got tighter. Cable replacement is the fix.

Common questions about home EV chargers in Perth

What changed for home EV chargers in November 2025?+

AS/NZS 3000 Amendment 3 took effect in November 2025 and added three new requirements for home EV charger installs. First, chargers above 20A single-phase must support demand-response so the network can ease load during peak times. Second, the charger must be on a dedicated circuit, separately RCD-protected (no more sharing with the oven, aircon or general power). Third, a switchboard capacity check is required before install — many Perth homes have 50A or 63A main supplies that can't handle an EV charger without an upgrade first. Older installs done before November 2025 weren't built to these rules, which is why many existing setups need retrofit.

What is demand-response and why does it matter for EV charging?+

Demand-response is a feature that lets the electricity network ease the load on your charger during peak demand periods (typically 5-9pm on hot summer days or cold winter mornings). The charger automatically reduces its draw or pauses charging until peak passes, then resumes. It protects the grid from overload as more EVs come online, and in some networks unlocks lower-cost off-peak charging rates. Since November 2025 it's mandatory on home chargers above 20A single-phase under AS/NZS 3000 Amendment 3. Modern chargers from Tesla, Fronius, ABB and Schneider all support it — older units may not.

Do I need a switchboard upgrade to install an EV charger?+

Sometimes — it depends on your main supply capacity and what else is running in the house. Many Perth homes have 50A or 63A main supplies, which is fine for normal household load but tight once you add a 32A EV charger on top of the existing aircon, oven, hot water and lighting. We do a capacity calculation as part of the phone price and confirm on site. If a switchboard upgrade is needed first, we coordinate the two jobs together. About 30-40% of older Perth homes need a board upgrade alongside the charger install.

Which brand of EV charger should I install at home?+

All four major brands we install (Tesla Wall Connector, Fronius Wattpilot, ABB Terra AC, Schneider EVlink) meet AS/NZS 3000 Amendment 3 and support demand-response. The choice usually comes down to features and ecosystem. Tesla Wall Connector is the natural pick if you own a Tesla — tightest integration with the car. Fronius Wattpilot pairs well if you have or plan solar (smart load-balancing with PV). ABB Terra AC is the workhorse choice for non-Tesla EVs and rental properties (simple, reliable, broad compatibility). Schneider EVlink suits households who want full smart-home integration with app control. We'll talk through the trade-offs when you ring — it's not a one-size-fits-all decision.

Can I install a home EV charger myself in WA?+

No. Under Western Australia's Electricity (Licensing) Regulations 1991, all 240V electrical work must be performed by a licensed electrician — and an EV charger install involves a dedicated circuit from the switchboard, RCD protection, capacity assessment and compliance certification. Unlicensed work is illegal, voids your home insurance and won't be certified to AS/NZS 3000 Amendment 3. We're licensed (EC009022) and issue a Notice of Completion and Electrical Safety Certificate on every install.

What if my existing EV charger was installed before November 2025?+

It was compliant with the rules at the time of install — you don't have to retrofit immediately. But it's worth checking whether the install would meet the current standard, especially if you're planning to sell the property, your insurance renews, or you're adding a second EV. Most retrofits involve adding RCD protection (if it was sharing a circuit), separating the charger onto its own circuit, and confirming the switchboard has enough capacity. Sometimes the charger itself can be reused; sometimes a swap to a demand-response-capable unit is the cleaner fix. Tell us the install date and the brand and we'll tell you over the phone what (if anything) needs updating.

How does the install process work?+

Phone price first — you tell us the car, the charger preference (or we recommend), where you want it mounted, and your switchboard situation. We size the whole install on the call and give you a fixed price the same day. We then visit, confirm the scope on site, and lock in the final price before any work begins. Install day involves running a dedicated cable from the switchboard to the charger location, mounting the unit, terminating to a new RCD-protected circuit, and commissioning it (testing, syncing with the network for demand-response, confirming compatibility with your car). You receive a Notice of Completion and Electrical Safety Certificate on the day.
