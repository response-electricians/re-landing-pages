The 30-second safety switch test every Perth home should do this month

Here’s a job for this weekend. Open your switchboard, find the buttons marked T or Test on your safety switches, press each one. The power should cut instantly. Reset, done — 30 seconds per switch. Most Perth homes have never done it. Some fail the test badly. Here’s exactly how to run it, what the outcomes mean, and what to do about it.

What a safety switch actually does

A safety switch — the same thing as an RCD, or Residual Current Device — is the device inside your switchboard that cuts the power in milliseconds if it detects electricity leaking to earth. Usually because it’s leaking through a person.

It’s the difference between a mild shock and a fatal one. Regular circuit breakers don’t do this job — they only protect the wiring from overload and short circuit, not people from shock. RCDs are the ones with a small button labelled T or Test on them.

The test button is there for a reason. It simulates a fault so you can prove the switch works before it’s ever needed for real. The government recommends every three months. Most Perth homes have never done it once.

How to run the 30-second test

1

Open your switchboard

Find the main switchboard — usually in the laundry, hallway, garage, or on an external wall. Open the cover door to expose the row of switches inside. You don’t need to touch anything else.

2

Find the T (Test) buttons

Look for switches with a small button labelled T or Test. These are your RCDs. Most modern boards have between two and eight of them. If you can’t find any at all, skip to Section 5.

3

Press each test button

Press the T button on one RCD. The switch should flip DOWN to the off position instantly, cutting power to that circuit. That’s a pass.

4

Reset the switch

Flip the switch back UP to restore power. Move to the next RCD and repeat. If it won’t reset after tripping, or didn’t trip at all, that’s a fail.

What a pass looks like

Simple. Press the button, the switch flips down instantly, power cuts to that circuit. That’s the RCD doing exactly what it’s designed to do. Reset the switch by flipping it back up, and move to the next one.

Every RCD that passes this test is proving it will save a life in the moment that matters — the split second between someone touching a live wire and their heart stopping. That’s not marketing language. That’s the actual physics of what these devices do.

What a failed test means

Two kinds of fail:

  • The switch doesn’t trip when you press the button. The RCD is dead. Its internal sensing mechanism has failed, usually from age. It won’t protect anyone. Replacement is a same-visit job for a licensed electrician.
  • The switch trips but won’t reset. Either the RCD itself is failing internally, or there’s an actual earth-leakage fault on that circuit (something plugged in is leaking to earth). Either way, don’t force the reset. Call us.

Don’t leave a failed RCD in place. It’s the same as leaving a smoke alarm with a dead battery — the moment you need it most is exactly when it won’t work.

If your switchboard has no test buttons at all

This is more common than you’d think in Perth. Any home whose switchboard predates the mid-1990s — and hasn’t been upgraded since — may have no RCD protection at all. Just ordinary circuit breakers, or even ceramic fuses.

Legally, an owner-occupied older home is allowed to have this setup. But under WA’s Electricity (Licensing) Regulations, RCDs have been required on any home sold or leased since 2009 — so if you haven’t moved recently, you may be missing them entirely.

What it looks like:

  • Round white ceramic fuse holders (looks like a stack of screws) — very old switchboard.
  • Rows of small toggle switches with no T buttons anywhere — older-style circuit breakers, no RCD protection.
  • Only one or two switches with T buttons, but many circuits without — some protection, but incomplete.

The fix is a switchboard upgrade — a full modern board with an RCD on every circuit. Usually a one-day job. See our switchboard upgrade guide for the full breakdown.

What WA law actually requires

Two rules to know:

  • Minimum two RCDs. Every home sold, leased or with electrical work done since 2009 must have at least two RCDs. Between them, they must cover every power point and lighting circuit.
  • Every three months, self-test. The WA government recommends testing every RCD in your home four times a year using the T button.

But — and this matters — the legal minimum leaves gaps. Two RCDs typically split the house into power circuits and lighting circuits, which leaves hard-wired appliances like the hot water system, oven and aircon on their own unprotected circuits. Those are exactly the appliances most likely to develop earth-leakage faults over time.

Our recommendation: an RCD on every final subcircuit, including hot water, oven, aircon, pool pumps and EV chargers. That’s what we fit as standard on new work and switchboard upgrades. The extra cost per RCD is small; the safety benefit is significant.

Insurance note

Most home insurance policies won’t pay out for electrical faults on circuits that were legally required to have RCD protection but didn’t. If you’ve recently bought an older home and haven’t verified RCD coverage, worth checking now — not after a claim.

Common questions about safety switch testing

How often should I test my safety switches?+

The WA government recommends testing every three months. That's four times a year. The test takes 30 seconds per switch, so a full home test rarely takes more than a few minutes. Most Perth homes have never done it once.

What's the difference between a safety switch, an RCD and a circuit breaker?+

A safety switch and an RCD are the same thing — RCD stands for Residual Current Device. It cuts power in milliseconds if it detects electricity leaking to earth (usually because it's flowing through a person). A circuit breaker is different — it protects the wiring from overload and short circuit, not people from shock. Most modern switchboards have both. You can tell an RCD by the T or Test button on it.

My switch trips when I press the test button. Is that bad?+

That's a pass. Tripping is exactly what the test is meant to prove — the RCD detected the simulated fault and cut the power. Reset the switch (flip it back up) and move to the next one. If it trips but won't reset, that's different — call us.

What does WA law require for safety switches?+

Under WA's Electricity (Licensing) Regulations 1991, all residential homes sold or leased since 2009 must have at least two RCDs protecting every power point and lighting circuit. Older homes that haven't changed hands may still be legal but underprotected. We recommend an RCD on every circuit (including hot water, oven and aircon) for full protection, and that's what we fit as standard.

My switchboard has no test buttons at all. What does that mean?+

It means your switchboard predates modern RCD requirements. Some or all of your circuits have no safety-switch protection. Common in Perth homes built before the mid-1990s that haven't had a switchboard upgrade. It's legal for owner-occupied older homes, but it's a genuine safety gap. A switchboard upgrade replaces the whole board with a modern one that has RCDs on every circuit.

How much does it cost to replace a failed RCD?+

We price the fix over the phone once you tell us how many RCDs need replacing and roughly which brand of switchboard you have. Fixed price, confirmed at the walkthrough, no surprises. If the whole board needs replacing (no test buttons, ceramic fuses still in place, or aged Clipsal 4-series), that's a switchboard upgrade — a bigger job but usually done in a day.

Can I replace a safety switch myself?+

No. Under WA's Electricity (Licensing) Regulations 1991, all switchboard work must be performed by a licensed electrician. Even a single RCD replacement requires opening the live board and terminating onto the bus bar. Unlicensed electrical work voids your home insurance and creates a serious safety hazard. We're licensed (EC009022) and issue an Electrical Safety Certificate on every job.
