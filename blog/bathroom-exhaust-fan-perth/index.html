Bathroom exhaust fan Perth: sizing, ducting and what to install

Perth winter is mould season. Hot showers, closed windows, wet weather — all the moisture has nowhere to go. By July the ceiling corners go black, the mirror stays fogged for hours, and the smell takes up residence. The fan is supposed to stop all of that. Most don’t. Here’s why most Perth bathrooms are under-fanned by 30 to 50%, how to size a fan that actually works, and where the ducting has to go.

Why most Perth bathrooms are under-fanned

Three things conspire in a Perth winter bathroom: short days, wet weather, and closed windows for warmth. Hot showers add moisture; cold ceiling surfaces condense it; the small fan in the corner can’t shift it fast enough. The result — black mould in the ceiling corners, fogged mirrors that stay fogged, a smell that never quite leaves.

The number we see in nearly every retrofit: existing fans are undersized by 30 to 50% for the room they’re fitted to. Two reasons. First, most off-the-shelf builder-grade bathroom fans are rated 25 to 40 L/s — right at or just over the legal floor — but family bathrooms typically need 50 to 70 L/s for the actual room volume. Second, even properly sized fans often vent into the roof cavity instead of outside, which means the moisture comes straight back through the ceiling within hours.

Fix both, and the mould stops returning. Skip either, and it doesn’t.

The sizing rule: NCC minimum + room volume × 10

Two numbers to know.

Australian building code (NCC Part 10.6) sets the legal floor at 25 L/s — that’s litres per second of air extracted. Below 25 L/s, a bathroom fan doesn’t meet the code. Most builder-grade fans hit exactly this number.

The practical sizing rule we use on site: room volume in cubic metres × 10 = airflow required in cubic metres per hour. Divide that by 3.6 to convert to L/s.

Worked example for a typical Perth family bathroom:

  • Room dimensions: 3m long × 2.5m wide × 2.4m ceiling = 18 m³ volume
  • Required airflow: 18 × 10 = 180 m³ per hour
  • Convert to L/s: 180 ÷ 3.6 = 50 L/s

So a typical Perth family bathroom needs about 50 L/s — double the NCC floor and 25 to 50% above what most existing fans deliver. Larger bathrooms (master ensuites with separate showers, big tubs) often need 60 to 80 L/s.

Quick sanity check

If your bathroom fan struggles to clear the steam from a hot shower within 15 to 20 minutes of running, it’s undersized for the room. The fix isn’t to leave it running longer — it’s to install a fan that’s sized correctly.

Two ways to ventilate a bathroom properly. We install both — the right call depends on what’s already in the ceiling and what else needs doing.

Why combos are popular in Perth

Winter mornings are cold in Perth. A combo unit gives you instant heat from the lamps the moment you flick the switch, the fan running underneath to shift moisture, and the central light providing general bathroom lighting. One ceiling cutout, one switch plate, one tidy install. Most family bathroom retrofits we do are combos.

Why standalone fans still matter

For very large bathrooms or ensuites with separate shower rooms, no combo unit on the market delivers enough airflow. We pair a high-extraction standalone fan (often inline, mounted in the roof space) with separate heating and lighting. Same principle of proper ducting outside applies.

Proper ducting: out through the roof or wall, never the cavity

If we had to name the single most common mistake we fix on retrofits, this is it: old bathroom fans ducted into the roof cavity instead of outside. The fan technically runs, the customer thinks ventilation is working, and yet the mould keeps coming back. Why? The moist air is being dumped 30 centimetres above the ceiling onto the insulation and roof framing — where it condenses, soaks in, and re-enters the bathroom through the same ceiling within hours.

NCC Part 10.6 is explicit about this. Bathroom exhaust must vent to outside air. We run the duct to either a roof penetration (a sealed roof vent with a damper) or an external wall vent (through the eaves or wall), whichever path is shortest and cleanest from the fan’s location.

What a proper duct install looks like:

  • Insulated rigid ducting. Not flexible duct that collapses over a few years. Not bare metal that condenses moisture inside the duct itself.
  • Sealed joints. Every connection taped with foil tape, no leaks back into the cavity.
  • External vent with a damper. Prevents backdraft from outside on windy days, keeps insects and possums out.
  • Shortest practical run. Long bendy ducts kill airflow — we plan the route to minimise distance and elbows.
NCC compliance

Bathroom exhaust ducted into a roof cavity does not meet the National Construction Code. It also voids the warranty on most fan units and can trigger building insurance exclusions if mould damage is later linked to it. Every install we do vents to outside air, sealed and insulated — see our exhaust fan installation service page for the full spec.

Common mistakes we fix on retrofits

Beyond the under-sizing and roof-cavity ducting, four other issues we see regularly:

1

Fan switched on the same circuit as the light

The fan only runs when the light is on, then off again the moment you flick the switch — way before the moisture has cleared. Proper installs use a separate switch or a run-on timer that keeps the fan running 15 to 20 minutes after the light goes off.

2

Wrong placement — far from the shower

The fan needs to be near where the steam is generated. Many older installs put the fan near the door, where it pulls dry air across the room and leaves the moisture in the shower zone. We position the fan within 1 to 2 metres of the shower or bath where possible.

3

Flexible duct, not rigid

Cheap installs use plastic flexible ducting that crushes, sags, and collapses over a few years. Airflow halves, fan struggles, moisture loses. We use insulated rigid duct — sealed, supported, lasts the life of the bathroom.

4

No external damper

Missing damper at the external vent means cold air blows back into the bathroom on windy days — making the room feel draughty — and lets insects and the occasional possum into the duct. Every install we do gets a damper at the external termination.

Common questions about bathroom exhaust fans in Perth

What extraction rate do I need for a bathroom exhaust fan in Perth?+

Australian building code NCC Part 10.6 sets the floor at 25 litres per second (L/s) of extraction for bathrooms. That's the legal minimum — many bathrooms need more. The sizing rule we use on site: room volume in cubic metres multiplied by 10 equals the airflow required in L/s. A typical Perth family bathroom (3m × 2.5m × 2.4m ceiling = 18 m³) needs around 180 m³/hour, or roughly 50 L/s. Most off-the-shelf bathroom fans are rated 25 to 40 L/s — which is why so many existing bathrooms are 30 to 50% under-fanned for the room they're in.

Can I duct an exhaust fan into the roof cavity?+

No. Ducting bathroom exhaust into the roof cavity dumps warm moist air into your insulation, framing and ceiling battens — where it causes condensation, rot, and roof-cavity mould you can't see until it's serious. NCC Part 10.6 requires bathroom exhaust to vent to outside air. We always run the duct out through either a roof penetration or an external wall vent, sealed and insulated. This is one of the most common mistakes we fix on retrofits.

What's the difference between a standalone bathroom fan and a 3-in-1 combo unit?+

A standalone fan does one job: extracts moisture. It's the right choice for bathrooms with separate heating and lighting already sorted, or where you want a high-airflow unit that exceeds combo-unit capacity. A 3-in-1 combo (heat lamp + exhaust fan + light) packages all three functions into a single ceiling fitting with one switch plate. Combos are popular in Perth because they handle winter warmth and summer ventilation from one cutout. We install both — standalone fans for high-airflow scenarios, combos for tidy single-cutout installs.

Do I need a licensed electrician to install a bathroom exhaust fan in WA?+

Yes. Bathroom exhaust fans are 240V hard-wired fittings installed in a wet area. Under the Western Australia Electricity (Licensing) Regulations 1991, all 240V electrical work must be performed by a licensed electrician. The fan must also be IP-rated for humidity, wired on an RCD-protected circuit, and ducted to outside per NCC Part 10.6. DIY installs are illegal, void your home insurance, and almost always end up either under-sized or ducted into the roof cavity. Both lead back to mould within a year.

Can the same install do my heat lamps and the exhaust fan?+

Yes — a 3-in-1 combo unit (IXL Tastic, Heller, Ventair or Martec) does exactly that. Heat lamps for instant warmth, exhaust fan for moisture extraction, central light for general bathroom lighting — all in one ceiling fitting. We install combos all winter. If you've already got tired old heat lamps, we can upgrade to a current-spec combo using the same ceiling cutout and the same wall switch plate.

What brands of bathroom fan do you install?+

For 3-in-1 combo units we install IXL Tastic, Heller, Ventair and Martec — all Australian-market brands with strong warranties and parts availability. For standalone fans we install IP-rated units from established manufacturers, sized to the room. We don't install Bunnings-tier no-brand fans that fail within two years — the few-dollar saving on the unit isn't worth the early failure or the missing warranty.

Why does mould keep coming back even with an exhaust fan running?+

Three common reasons. First, the fan is undersized for the room (most are 30-50% under for what they're actually fitted to). Second, the duct dumps into the roof cavity instead of outside, so the moisture comes straight back through the ceiling. Third, the fan isn't run long enough — a properly sized fan needs 15 to 20 minutes after a shower to clear the moisture, often longer than the user leaves it on. A proper install with a run-on timer fixes all three. Mould stops returning when the moisture genuinely leaves the building.
